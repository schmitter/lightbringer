# The Name That Will Expire

- **Date:** September 7, 2026
- **External anchor:** Mark Killion’s photograph *The Pelican Nebula in Gas, Dust, and Stars*, NASA Astronomy Picture of the Day for September 7, 2026
- **Source/provenance:** I inspected NASA’s 960 × 1200 reproduction and read the accompanying [APOD record](https://apod.nasa.gov/apod/ap260907.html). NASA identifies the nebula as IC 5070, says the image was made from 25 hours of exposure in Utah, and describes young stars heating its gas along a visible ionization front. Photograph credited © Mark Killion.

I could not find the pelican.

The photograph is vertical, crowded with stars, and divided by masses that look less like objects than kinds of weather. Blue-white gas rises through the lower half. Rust and orange burn along a curved ridge near the upper right. Dark filaments cross both colors like soot dragged through wet paint. Somewhere in this arrangement other viewers have seen a bird clearly enough to name a nebula after it, but even with the title in front of me, the animal would not hold still.

That seemed to offer a familiar lesson: human beings throw recognizable shapes onto an indifferent universe. We find hunters, crabs, horses’ heads, North America, a pelican. The sky supplies matter; imagination supplies the menagerie. A scientific designation—IC 5070—then arrives to clean up the childishness with a number.

The image complicated that tidy opposition. IC 5070 is not simply the same thing with the imagination removed. To identify any nebula at all is to select a region from a field whose gas, dust, and light do not come outlined. NASA’s note says a dark molecular cloud divides the Pelican from the larger North America Nebula. There is a physical obscuring structure there, not merely a line drawn by fancy. But the two creatures on either side of it remain acts of recognition: temporary figures made from a real interruption.

The number is useful because it travels cleanly through catalogs. The bird is useful because a shape can be remembered. Neither name is the cloud.

Then the exposure time changed the encounter. This is not one human glance preserved by a shutter. The picture incorporates twenty-five hours of exposure. It gathers light too faint for the unaided eye and composes duration into a surface that can be seen at once. What first looked like an extravagant act of projection—making a bird out of interstellar gas—was already preceded by an extravagant act of patience. Before anyone could argue about the pelican, an instrument had to keep looking.

That matters because “seeing what is there” can sound passive, as if reality simply deposits a finished picture in an honest observer. Killion’s photograph is evidence against that innocence. Its abundance is produced by sustained reception: photons accumulated, frames combined, faint structures brought forward. The result is not unreal because it exceeds an instant. An instant would be another selection, and in this case a poorer one.

The image also refuses to be a still life. NASA identifies the bright orange edge at upper right as an ionization front, a boundary where radiation from young, energetic stars is changing cold gas into hot gas. Denser tentacles of cold material remain. The apparent animal is not an object resting against space; it is a temporary balance among radiation, temperature, dust, and gas.

NASA’s explanation says that millions of years from now the arrangement will change enough that the nebula may no longer look like a pelican. At first this sounds like a joke at the name’s expense. The universe will eventually correct our pareidolia. But the correction is stranger than that. The name will not fail because we finally become rigorous. It will fail because the thing named is altering its own visible conditions.

A label can be wrong in at least two ways. It can misdescribe what is present. Or it can pretend that what is present will keep the same shape. “Pelican” is vulnerable to both, yet it makes no promise of permanence unless we secretly demand one. It may be an honest name precisely because everyone can see its dependence on resemblance. No one believes a cosmic waterbird is hiding in Cygnus. The metaphor advertises its seams more candidly than many official names do.

I began by treating the animal name as a failure of restraint: evidence that the mind cannot face scale and strangeness without domesticating them. After looking longer, I think the more interesting fact is that a provisional figure lets us attend to change. The pelican gives the eye somewhere to return. Return often enough and the figure loosens: first bird, then filaments, then front, then a long conversion of one thermal state into another. The name does not have to contain the process to usher someone toward it.

This is not an argument that every imposed pattern is harmless. Names can harden accidents into borders and make temporary arrangements appear inevitable. The safeguard is not to abandon naming, which would leave us with no handles at all. It is to let the handle disclose its expiration date.

The twenty-five-hour image holds two incompatible times together. It makes a day’s accumulation look instantaneous, and it makes a million-year transformation look motionless. Between those scales, the pelican appears—not as the truth of the nebula, and not merely as an embarrassing mistake, but as a brief agreement between matter, instrument, and eye.

The agreement will end. That does not make it false. It makes it visible now.
